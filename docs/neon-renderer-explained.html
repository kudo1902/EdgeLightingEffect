Anatomy of a Neon Edge

How one shader turns a rectangle into a glowing tube of light, without ever drawing a tube - and how the rain and lens flare stacked around it reuse the same two ideas. Written for people who do not write graphics code.

The whole thing, in five steps

  1. The renderer never builds a glowing shape. It covers the area with a plain rectangle of pixels and asks each pixel two questions.
  2. Question one: how far am I from the outline? One number, in pixels, computed with a short formula. Negative inside, zero on the line, positive outside.
  3. Question two: where along the outline am I? A number from 0 to 1 that walks once around the perimeter. This is what makes moving lights and multi-colour gradients possible.
  4. Distance picks the brightness (three stacked layers: a hard filament, a tight halo, a wide bloom). Position picks the colour and decides whether this stretch of the ring is lit at all.
  5. Multiply them, compress the highlights, and blend the result over whatever was already on screen.

01 Nobody draws the tube

The instinct is to build geometry: a ribbon of triangles bent around the rectangle, textured to look like glass. This renderer does the opposite, and that choice explains almost everything else about it.

Instead of building a shape, it draws one flat rectangle covering the area where light might land, and hands the GPU a small program - a fragment shader - that runs once for every pixel inside it, all in parallel. That program's job is to answer a single question: how bright and what colour is this one pixel?

To answer it, the pixel needs to know how far it is from the outline it is supposed to be glowing around. That distance is not looked up in a texture or measured against a list of line segments. It comes out of a formula about six lines long, evaluated fresh for every pixel.

This is called a signed distance field, and it is worth understanding one property of it before going further, because several design decisions fall directly out of it.

02 The field, and why corners are free

If you colour every pixel by its distance, you get a set of nested rings. For a rounded rectangle those rings have a convenient shape: each one is itself a rounded rectangle, grown outward with a proportionally larger corner radius.

That is the payoff. A triangle-based approach has to stitch corner geometry, worry about the seams where straight edges meet arcs, and re-tune every time the corner radius changes. Here, the corner is not a case in the code at all. It falls out of the arithmetic.

The formula also gives you the sign for free, which is how the "glow inside only" and "glow outside only" modes work: they are a single comparison against zero.

Under the hood: the distance formula

Six lines, in lib/shaders/neon.frag:

float sdRoundBox(vec2 p, vec2 b, float r) {
    vec2 q = abs(p) - b + r;
    return min(max(q.x, q.y), 0.0) + length(max(q, 0.0)) - r;
}

p is the pixel's position relative to the rectangle's centre, b is the rectangle's half-width and half-height, r is the corner radius.

  • abs(p) folds the plane into one quadrant. The shape is symmetric, so one quadrant of maths covers all four.
  • - b + r shifts into the frame of a smaller, sharp-cornered box. A rounded rectangle is exactly that smaller box with a disc of radius r rolled around it - which is why subtracting r at the end recovers the rounded shape.
  • length(max(q, 0.0)) handles pixels outside: clamping the negatives to zero projects onto the smaller box, so this is the straight-line distance to it.
  • min(max(q.x, q.y), 0.0) handles pixels inside, where distance to the nearest wall is the larger (least negative) of the two axis distances.

The result is an exact distance, not an approximation. That matters later: it means d can be compared directly against pixel-valued settings like lineWidth and glowRadius with no conversion.

03 The second question: where along the ring?

Distance alone gets you a uniform glow. It cannot give you a gradient that sweeps around the perimeter, an arc that lights only the top edge, or a bright spot travelling around the border - because all of those depend on where along the outline a pixel sits, not just how far from it.

So the shader computes a second number, t: walk once around the perimeter starting from the top-left corner, and t is how far along you are, from 0.0 back round to 1.0.

Getting t right is fiddlier than getting d right. The shader finds the nearest point on the outline geometrically, works out which of the eight pieces it landed on (four straight edges, four corner arcs), and converts that to a fraction of the total perimeter.

Why the effort An earlier version estimated t by averaging the positions of nearby sample points. It broke at corners: the samples there wrap through the start of the ring, so the average collapsed toward zero and lit corners that should have stayed dark. Measuring the position geometrically is exact everywhere, corners included.
Under the hood: finding the nearest point

The first three lines are the distance formula run backwards - instead of returning how far the nearest point is, they return where it is:

vec2  b  = vec2(halfWs, halfHs);   // the smaller, sharp-cornered box
vec2  c  = clamp(p, -b, b);        // nearest point on that box
vec2  d  = p - c;
cp = c + d * (r / length(d));      // push out by r -> on the outline

From cp, a chain of comparisons decides which of the eight perimeter pieces it belongs to. Straight edges give the fraction directly from the coordinate; corner arcs use the angle around the corner's centre. A pair of lookup tables then converts (piece, fraction) into a distance along the perimeter, one table per winding direction, and dividing by the perimeter length gives t.

This matches the CPU-side GeometryUtils::GetPointOnRectangle exactly, which is what lets settings authored on the CPU (an arc from 0.25 to 0.5, say) line up with what the shader draws.

04 Three layers of light

A real neon tube is not one glow. It is a searing filament you cannot look at, a tight coloured halo hugging the glass, and a wide soft wash spilling onto whatever is behind it. The shader builds all three separately and adds them up.

Each layer is a curve: feed in the distance d, get out a brightness. They differ almost entirely in how quickly they fall off.

LayerShapeWidth comes fromMultiplied by
Filament Bell curve, very steep lineWidth 12.0
Halo Falls off as 1 / distance² glowRadius 0.9
Bloom Falls off as 1 / distance glowRadius × 6 bloomStrength

The last column is why the filament reads as white-hot even though all three curves peak at 1.0: it is multiplied by twelve, so its centre blows far past what the screen can show and gets compressed back down at the end. That compression is what turns a coloured curve into a white core with coloured edges - the same thing a camera does when it photographs a real neon sign.

Why these are formulas and not sums

An earlier version computed the halo and bloom by adding up the contribution of 128 sample points spread around the perimeter. It worked, but it had a flaw that took a while to pin down: with only 128 points, a narrow glow beads - it breaks into a dotted line instead of a smooth ribbon. The fix at the time was to force the glow to be at least as wide as the gap between samples.

That gap depends on the size of the rectangle. So the glow's width, and therefore its brightness, silently tracked how big the rectangle was, and the glowRadius setting did nothing at all until it exceeded that floor - on a 1920x1080 rectangle, that was most of its usable range.

Replacing the sums with their exact mathematical limit removed the problem at the root. A formula has no samples, so it cannot bead at any width, so no floor is needed, so glowRadius now means the same thing on every rectangle.

Under the hood: the closed forms

Take a straight emitting line, a pixel at perpendicular distance ad, and let x run along the line. The old loop summed a kernel over x; letting the sample spacing go to zero turns each sum into an integral with an exact answer.

halo:   ∫ (x² + A²)^-3/2 dx = 2 / A²     with A² = ad² + kh²
bloom:  ∫ 1 / (x² + A²)  dx = π / A       with A  = sqrt(ad² + bw²)

which are exactly the two lines in the shader:

float halo  = HALO_NORM_FACTOR  * 2.0 * kh * kh / (ad * ad + kh * kh);
float bloom = BLOOM_NORM_FACTOR * PI * bw / sqrt(ad * ad + bw * bw);

The peak values at ad = 0 come out to 0.86 and 1.005 - identical to what the sums produced - so the calibration constants kept their meaning and nothing had to be re-tuned by eye.

One honest regression. On the inside of a corner the old sum picked up light from both incident edges, so corners ran about 40% hotter. A formula based on distance to the nearest point cannot reproduce that. The gap shrinks as the glow gets small relative to the corner radius, and vanishes entirely at corner radius 0.

05 Colour is a strip, baked in advance

Colours are authored as stops: a position around the ring and a colour. The default configuration has four - red at 0, green at a quarter, blue at halfway, yellow at three-quarters - and the ring blends between them, wrapping from the last back to the first.

Blending between stops is not free. Doing it inside the shader means every pixel walks the stop list and interpolates, every frame. So the renderer does it once on the CPU instead, writing the finished gradient into a 256-pixel-wide texture. The shader then does a single texture read: "what colour is at position t?"

Two details of the strip are deliberate:

  • It repeats. The texture wraps, so reading past the right edge comes back at the left. That is what makes the rotating-hue animation a single addition: add time to the read position and the whole ring sweeps, with no wrap-around logic anywhere.
  • It is 8-bit, not floating point. Float textures are not universally supported on lower-end and embedded GPUs, which this library targets. Every lookup table here is plain 8-bit RGBA for that reason.

The same trick is used twice more. Individual arcs and travelling segments can carry their own gradients, so there are two further textures - one row per arc, one row per segment - baked the same way.

A nice consequence Because the strip is a texture, changing colours can be animated for free: the renderer keeps the old strip and the new one and cross-fades between them over colorTransitionDuration seconds, re-uploading a blended strip each frame. The shader is not involved and does not know it is happening.

06 What happens each frame

With the ideas in place, here is the actual machinery. The effect orchestrator calls the renderer through three entry points, each with a distinct job. Two of them run every frame; the third runs only when the settings move.

The three entry points

OnConfigChanged Only when the settings change Compares the incoming settings against the last set it saw and rebuilds only what genuinely changed: perimeter points, the draw quad, the colour strip, the arc and segment atlases. 07 Inside the shader, for one pixel

Everything above converges here. This runs, independently, for every pixel the quad covers - hundreds of thousands of times per frame, all at once.

The three finishing steps

  • Tone map. The filament's brightness runs far past 1.0, which a screen cannot show. Rather than clipping each of red, green and blue separately - which desaturates warm colours, turning orange into peach as red saturates first - the shader compresses the brightest channel and scales the other two by the same ratio, preserving the hue.
  • Gamma. A small exponent that lifts the mid-tones, so the glow's falloff reads the way the eye expects rather than the way the arithmetic produces.
  • Premultiplied alpha. The pixel's opacity is set to its brightest channel. This is what lets the effect sit over arbitrary content: the white-hot core is nearly opaque and covers what is behind it like a real glass tube, while the faint outer bloom is nearly transparent and simply adds light.

08 The quad is bigger than the rectangle

One practical detail that trips people up when they first read the code: the rectangle drawn on screen is noticeably larger than the rectangle in the settings. It has to be. The bloom spreads a long way, and any pixel outside the drawn quad simply never runs the shader - it would be cut off with a visible straight edge.

Two details worth noting, because both look like mistakes at first glance:

  • The quad has sharp corners, and so does the fade. Everything else in the shader works on rounded contours, but what the fade is hiding is the quad, and the quad is a plain rectangle. Keying the fade off the rounded distance instead ate the corners early - it erased the outer 30% of every corner on a square-cornered configuration.
  • The bloom is deliberately pulled down to zero at the quad edge. The bloom's tail is heavy: at the quad's boundary it is still around 10% of peak. So the shader subtracts a small constant sized to make it land exactly at zero there, and rescales so the centre brightness is unchanged. The tail is very slightly shortened; nothing visible is lost, and the quad can stay small.

10 The same effect, at a quarter of the cost

The bloom covers a large area, and every pixel in it runs the whole shader. But most of that area is a smooth gradient with no fine detail in it - exactly the kind of thing you can compute small and stretch back up without anyone noticing.

That is what NeonOptimizedRenderer is. It draws the neon into an offscreen buffer at half the width and half the height, then scales the result onto the screen. Half in each direction is a quarter of the pixels, so the expensive per-pixel work drops by roughly four times, at the price of one cheap full-screen copy.

Scaling a glow back up would normally leave dark fringes around it. It does not here, and the reason is a decision made back in Section 7: the shader outputs premultiplied alpha, where colour and coverage are already scaled together. Averaging two premultiplied pixels gives the correct answer, so plain bilinear filtering is enough and the copy shader is two lines long.

Why the shader is almost unchanged

Inside the offscreen buffer, everything is half size. Rather than teach the shader about that, the CPU multiplies every measurement by the scale before uploading it: rectangle size, corner radius, line width, glow radius, the cutoffs. The shader then works entirely in buffer pixels and never knows the difference.

That works because of a property of the distance formula worth stating on its own: halve every input and the distance halves too. Every comparison in the shader is a ratio of two lengths, and both halve together, so every ratio is unchanged.

There is one category of exception, and it is easy to state: a constant written into the shader cannot scale itself. Anywhere a fixed pixel measurement from the tuning file meets a value the CPU has already scaled, the optimized copy has to bring it into the same units.

The rule falls out of the table: a constant measured in pixels needs the correction; a constant that is a plain fraction does not, because the value it multiplies is already in buffer pixels. Miss one and the symptom is geometric rather than a crash - the arc feathers without their correction inset each arc's filament twice as deep as the full-resolution renderer does, leaving a visible stretch of bare halo past the ends.

A second knob: fewer samples

The optimized renderer also exposes numSamples, defaulting to 64 of the available 128. This changes one thing only: how many perimeter points the colour gather visits.

It is safe to turn down precisely because of the rewrite described in Section 4. Brightness, coverage and the glow's reach are all computed from formulas now, and none of them touch the sample list. Halving the samples costs a little precision in how colours blend where two stops meet, and nothing else - no dimming, no change in glow width, no beading.

What it actually costs

  • The filament is what suffers. The wide, soft layers survive the round trip essentially untouched - that is the whole premise. But the filament is a 4 px razor-sharp line; at half resolution it is drawn 2 px wide and then blurred back up. Wide, soft configurations are nearly free to optimize. Thin, bright, hard-edged ones are not.
  • It is a near-fork, not a wrapper. Two C++ files and two shader files hold near-identical code. A change to how the neon looks has to land in both copies or the two renderers quietly drift apart.
  • Do not enable both at once. They read the same config.neon for appearance but have separate switches (neon.enable and optimizedNeon.enable). Turning both on draws the effect twice, on top of itself.

Under the hood: scaling the uniforms

Every geometric uniform is pre-multiplied on the CPU, in lib/src/renderer/neon-optimized-renderer.cpp:

mNeonShader.SetUniform("uResolutionScale", scale);
mNeonShader.SetUniform("uRectSize",     rectSizeScaled);

Note what is not scaled: intensity, bloomStrength, filamentFalloff and the arc and segment positions. The first three are unitless multipliers; the last are positions around the perimeter expressed from 0 to 1, so they mean the same thing at any size.

uResolutionScale is uploaded purely so the shader can convert the handful of baked-in pixel constants from the table above. It is the only uniform in the pair of shaders that exists because of the optimization.

The two-pass structure also has to be careful about where it returns to. A frame capture binds a real offscreen target rather than the window, so pass 2 restores whatever framebuffer the renderer was handed:

const GLuint targetFbo = Framebuffer::GetBoundId();   // before pass 1
...
Framebuffer::BindId(targetFbo);                       // before pass 2

Binding the default framebuffer instead would send the composite to the window during a capture, and the captured image would come back empty.

11 Six layers over one rectangle

Everything so far has been a single renderer. The effect is actually a list of them, and the neon is only the one that gets the most attention.

The orchestrator holds a list of renderers and, every frame, calls each one in turn. There is no scene graph and no depth sorting: each renderer draws over whatever the previous one left behind, and the order in the list is the stacking order. Any subset can be switched on.

There is a thread running through them. Three of the six - neon, droplets and lens flare - build their geometry out of the two measurements from Sections 2 and 3: the signed distance to the outline and the position around the perimeter. Learning those two ideas once buys you most of the way into all three.

The wireframe, in one paragraph

The simplest renderer in the library, and worth mentioning only because of one detail. It uploads four corner points, draws them as a closed loop of 1-pixel lines in a flat colour, and turns blending off while it does so. It is on by default, in green.

The detail: it draws the sharp rectangle. It has no corner radius, because it is the reference you check the other layers against - where the geometry says the box is, before any rounding, glow or feathering. If the neon looks offset from the wireframe, that is a genuine bug; if the neon's corners curve inside it, that is just the corner radius doing its job.

12 Rain on glass

The droplets layer puts running water in a band that hugs the outline. It looks like a fluid simulation. It is not - it is a hashed grid, masked by the same distance formula the neon uses.

The band is defined entirely by distance. Take d from Section 2, measure how deep into the band the pixel is, and divide by the band width to get a number that is 0 at the band's inner boundary and 1 at its outer. Everything the droplet field does is parameterised on that. Which side of the outline the band sits on is a setting.

Rain falls down, not around

The tempting move is to make the droplets flow along the perimeter, since that is the shape the effect is built around. It was tried and it looks wrong - water circulating around a loop reads as a lava lamp, not as rain.

So gravity is global and points straight down the screen, and the droplet grid is hashed in screen space. The perimeter controls only two things: where drops are allowed to show, and how big they are.

That creates a problem the shader has to solve. A band is not uniformly oriented: its left and right stretches run parallel to gravity, and its top and bottom run across it. Real rain does not streak sideways.

Two details that took a while to get right

  • Drop size comes from the band, not the screen. Originally the droplet cells were sized from the viewport, so a 20-pixel band showed slivers of drops that were tens of pixels across - you saw fragments of enormous droplets, not small ones. Deriving the cell size from the band width instead means drops fit the band however thin you make it.
  • Water has no colour of its own. A drop is visible only through what it does to the light around it. So the body of each drop stays about 90% transparent, and the two things that actually read as water are drawn on top: a crescent along the drop's edge, weighted toward the light so it does not look like a soap bubble, and one tight specular dot. An earlier version derived the crescent from the height-field slope, which saturates across the whole drop at any usable brightness and filled every drop solid white; taking it from the drop's own coverage instead keeps it a thin outline at any size.
Not in there There is no refraction and no framebuffer capture. Both existed once. The only backdrop this band ever sits on is the neon's own smooth gradient, and refraction had nothing to displace against it - the effect was invisible, so the whole capture path was removed. What is left is self-lit highlights, which cost nothing and read the same.

13 The lens flare

The last layer simulates a camera looking at a bright light: not the light itself so much as everything the lens adds on the way to the sensor.

It is one full-screen pass with no geometry at all. Every pixel works out its distance and angle from the sun and evaluates a stack of terms. And the sun is not placed in screen coordinates - it rides the perimeter, in the same 0-to-1 parameter space the neon arcs use, with a signed offset that pushes it outside the rectangle or pulls it inside.

Riding the perimeter has one subtlety worth knowing. The sun does not follow the rectangle and then get nudged outward - it follows the offset curve directly, which is itself a rounded rectangle with a larger radius. Because the position advances by arc length along that curve, the sun travels at a constant speed through the corners instead of visibly slowing down at them.

The three groups of terms

  • Ghosts. Ten groups spaced along the axis, each made of a soft blob, a thin ring and a hexagonal sprite. Their colours come from a procedural palette that shifts with distance, so no two read the same - and can be blended toward a single colour if the rainbow is too much.
  • Rays. A set of thin spikes, plus fainter needles at the half-angles between them, plus a per-ray random length. The random length is what stops it looking like a flower: without it every spike is the same length and the sunburst reads as a decoration rather than a light.
  • Core, haze and glow. Three falloffs stacked at the sun's centre, all faded out by a soft envelope with distance, which is what keeps the flare from covering the whole screen.
A familiar trap The falloff terms started as pure power laws, which are scale free: dividing the radius by a size factor is algebraically the same as multiplying the whole term by that factor raised to a power. The size slider was therefore also a brightness slider, and a badly non-linear one - the core term scaled with size to the fifth power. Adding a small floor to each falloff gives it a finite peak, so size sets the radius and intensity sets the brightness, independently. It is the same class of problem as the neon's beading floor in Section 4: a value that looked like one control was quietly acting as two.

Like the neon, the flare has a half-resolution twin that renders into a scaled buffer and composites back, using exactly the mechanism from Section 10. And with the same caveat: the two draw the same flare, so switching both on gives you two of them.

Worth knowing Shaders are not loaded from disk at runtime. At build time CMake reads the .frag and .vert files and pastes them into a generated header as string constants, alongside the tuning header. Editing a shader therefore requires a rebuild - but it also means there is no file I/O, no shader directory to ship, and no way for the constants in the shader to disagree with the constants in the C++.

The two shader files are the pair to watch. They are close to identical by design, and the build system cannot tell you when they have drifted - only the eye can. When changing how the neon looks, change both.
