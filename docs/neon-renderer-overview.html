Neon Edge in Brief

The whole renderer in five minutes: what it draws, the two numbers everything is built from, and where the other layers fit. No graphics background assumed.

The whole thing, in five steps

  1. The renderer never builds a glowing shape. It covers the area with a plain rectangle of pixels and asks each pixel two questions.
  2. Question one: how far am I from the outline? One number, in pixels, computed with a short formula. Negative inside, zero on the line, positive outside.
  3. Question two: where along the outline am I? A number from 0 to 1 that walks once around the perimeter. This is what makes moving lights and multi-colour gradients possible.
  4. Distance picks the brightness (three stacked layers: a hard filament, a tight halo, a wide bloom). Position picks the colour and decides whether this stretch of the ring is lit at all.
  5. Multiply them, compress the highlights, and blend the result over whatever was already on screen.

01 One quad, two questions

The instinct is to build geometry: a ribbon of triangles bent around the rectangle, textured to look like glass. This renderer does the opposite, and that choice explains almost everything else about it.

It draws one flat rectangle covering the area where light might land, and hands the GPU a small program - a fragment shader - that runs once for every pixel inside it, all in parallel. That program answers a single question: how bright and what colour is this one pixel?

To answer it, each pixel computes two independent numbers from its own position.

d comes out of a six-line formula (a signed distance field) rather than a texture lookup or a search through line segments. That has one consequence worth knowing up front: every distance contour around a rounded rectangle is itself a rounded rectangle, so corners need no special handling anywhere in the shader. A glow that spreads 40 px follows the corner curve automatically, at the correct width. A triangle-based approach has to stitch corner geometry and re-tune whenever the corner radius changes.

t is the fiddlier of the two. The shader finds the nearest point on the outline, works out which of the eight pieces it landed on (four straight edges, four corner arcs), and converts that to a fraction of the total perimeter. It matches the CPU's GeometryUtils::GetPointOnRectangle exactly, which is what lets a setting authored on the CPU - an arc from 0.25 to 0.5, say - line up with what the shader draws.

02 Three layers of light

A real neon tube is not one glow. It is a searing filament you cannot look at, a tight coloured halo hugging the glass, and a wide soft wash spilling onto whatever is behind it. The shader builds all three from d separately and adds them up. They differ almost entirely in how quickly they fall off.

The last column is why the filament reads as white-hot even though all three curves peak at 1.0: it is multiplied by twelve, so its centre blows far past what the screen can show and gets compressed back down at the end. That compression turns a coloured curve into a white core with coloured edges, the same thing a camera does when it photographs a real neon sign.

All three are formulas, not sums. An earlier version added up the contribution of 128 sample points around the perimeter, which meant a narrow glow broke into a dotted line and had to be floored at the sample spacing - and that spacing depends on the rectangle's size, so glowRadius silently did nothing until it exceeded the floor. Replacing the sums with their exact mathematical limit removed the problem at the root.

03 Colour is a strip, baked in advance

Colours are authored as stops: a position around the ring and a colour. Blending between them inside the shader would mean every pixel walks the stop list every frame, so the renderer does it once on the CPU instead and writes the finished gradient into a 256-pixel-wide texture. The shader then does a single read: "what colour is at position t?"

Two properties of the strip are deliberate:

  • It repeats. Reading past the right edge comes back at the left, which makes the rotating-hue animation a single addition: add time to the read position and the whole ring sweeps, with no wrap-around logic anywhere.
  • It is 8-bit, not floating point. Float textures are not universally supported on lower-end and embedded GPUs, which this library targets. Every lookup table here is plain 8-bit RGBA for that reason.

The same trick is used twice more, for arcs and travelling segments that carry their own gradients: one atlas texture each, one row per arc or per segment.

A nice consequence Because the strip is a texture, a colour change can be animated for free. The renderer keeps the old strip and the new one and cross-fades between them over colorTransitionDuration seconds, re-uploading a blended strip each frame. The shader is not involved and does not know it is happening.

04 Six layers over one rectangle

The neon is one renderer among six. The orchestrator holds a list and calls each in turn every frame. There is no scene graph and no depth sorting: each renderer draws over whatever the previous one left behind, the order in the list is the stacking order, and any subset can be switched on.

There is a thread running through them. Three of the six - neon, droplets and lens flare - build their geometry out of the same two measurements from Section 1:

  • Droplets use d to carve a band hugging the outline, then fill it with a hashed grid of drops. Gravity is global and points straight down the screen, so drops streak on the band's vertical stretches and bead on its horizontal ones. It looks like a fluid simulation; it is a masked noise field.
  • Lens flare puts a sun at a position t around the perimeter and evaluates a stack of camera artifacts (hex ghosts, rays, core) from each pixel's distance and angle to it. One fullscreen pass, no geometry at all.

Learning d and t once buys you most of the way into all three.

05 Drawing at half resolution

The bloom covers a large area and every pixel in it runs the whole shader, but most of that area is a smooth gradient with no fine detail. So the neon renderer can draw into an offscreen buffer at half width and half height, then scale the result up - set resolutionScale below 1.0 and that is the path it takes. Half in each direction is a quarter of the pixels, so the per-pixel work drops roughly fourfold at the price of one cheap fullscreen copy. At 1.0 it skips the buffer entirely and draws straight onto the target.

Two facts make this nearly free:

  • Premultiplied alpha survives averaging. The shader outputs colour and coverage already scaled together, so plain bilinear filtering gives the correct answer and the copy shader is two lines long. Scaling a glow up would otherwise leave dark fringes.
  • The maths is scale-free. Every comparison in the shader is a ratio of two lengths. Halve every input and both halve together, so the CPU can simply multiply each measurement by the scale before uploading and the shader never learns the difference.

What it costs is the filament. The wide soft layers survive the round trip essentially untouched, but a 4 px razor-sharp line drawn 2 px wide and blurred back up does not. Wide, soft configurations are nearly free to optimize; thin, bright, hard-edged ones are not.

Watch out The lens flare still keeps a separate *Optimized renderer, a near-fork in both the .cpp and the .frag sharing one visual config - a change to how the flare looks has to land in both copies, and the build system cannot tell you when they have drifted. The neon layer used to work that way too; it now scales inside one renderer and one shader, so there is no second copy to forget.
